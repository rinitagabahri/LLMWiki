# Always Coach, or Match the Style: GROW vs. Hersey-Blanchard

**Faced with the same task — how do I engage this person to help them progress? — the two models give opposite advice. GROW says *coach*: ask, don't tell, draw the answer out of them. Hersey-Blanchard says coaching is only one of four styles, and the wrong one for a novice or an expert. The clash resolves on a single variable: the person's readiness on the task.**

## The clash

| | [[john-whitmore-mode]] (GROW) | [[hersey-blanchard-model]] |
|---|---|---|
| **Default move** | Coach — ask questions, generate their own options | Depends — instruct, coach, support, *or* delegate |
| **On a keen novice** | Still coach: draw it out of them | **Instruct** — questions leave a beginner floundering |
| **On a self-reliant expert** | Still coach: ask, don't tell | **Delegate** — don't even coach, get out of the way |
| **What it optimises** | Ownership — the person commits to answers they found | Fit — the amount of direction the person actually needs |
| **Origin** | John Whitmore (GROW) | Hersey & Blanchard (Situational Leadership) |

## Why they seem to contradict

GROW's whole gift is that it *refuses to let you tell someone the answer.* The Options step demands the person generate at least three real alternatives themselves; the coach only asks. That's powerful — but it assumes the person *has* answers to draw out. Hersey-Blanchard says that assumption breaks at both ends of the readiness scale: a keen but unskilled beginner has no answers to surface (asking them "what are your options?" is cruelty, not coaching), and a self-reliant expert doesn't need the conversation at all (coaching them is just delay). So "always coach" collides head-on with "coach only the disillusioned learner."

## How they reconcile — on readiness

The deciding variable is **the person's readiness on this task — their competence combined with their commitment**:

- **Low competence (a beginner)** → **Instruct.** Give steps, not questions. GROW's ask-don't-tell would leave them stuck.
- **Some competence, low commitment (disillusioned learner)** → **Coach.** *This* is where GROW is exactly right: they have enough to draw on, and questions rebuild both skill and ownership.
- **High competence, low confidence** → **Support.** Fewer questions about the *how*, more encouragement to trust their own judgement.
- **High competence and commitment (expert)** → **Delegate.** Set the goal and leave. Coaching here wastes everyone's time.

So GROW isn't wrong — it's the right tool for *one* of the four readiness levels. Read as a universal default it over-coaches beginners and experts alike; read as H-B's style 2, it's the sharpest coaching method in the book.

## The synthesis

Diagnose readiness first (Hersey-Blanchard), *then* pick the engagement. When the person lands in the "some skill, needs to own it" zone, reach for GROW and coach properly. **Match the style to the person, and use GROW when — but only when — coaching is the style the moment calls for.**
