# The Conflict Resolution Model

**In any conflict you have six possible responses — naming them turns an unconscious reaction into a deliberate choice.**

| # | Response | What it looks like | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 🏃 **Flight** | Avoid, leave the room, pretend it isn't there | **lose / lose** — issue festers, both suffer |
| 2 | 🥊 **Fight** | Argue, escalate, win-at-all-costs | **win / lose** — you win, they lose (and remember) |
| 3 | 🙇 **Give up** | Cave, surrender, suppress yourself | **lose / win** — they win, you lose (resentment builds) |
| 4 | 🫥 **Evade responsibility** | Punt the decision to a higher authority (manager, lead) | **lose / lose** — neither owns the outcome; the manager's call may not fit either of you |
| 5 | 🤝 **Compromise** | Both sides give up something | **partial / partial** — both half-win, half-lose; workable but no one's fully happy |
| 6 | 🎯 **Reach consensus** | A solution that genuinely works for both sides | **win / win** — the elegant resolution |

## When to Use It

- Mid-conflict — catch yourself defaulting to flight or fight and pause
- After a conflict ended badly — audit which mode you used and why
- Before entering a known conflict (a hard 1:1, a tense meeting) — pre-decide your stance
- When you notice you keep using the *same* mode in every conflict (your autopilot)

## Key Insight

Most people have one or two **default modes** (always flight, always fight, always compromise) and use them regardless of the situation. The model breaks the autopilot. **Consensus is the elegant goal, but compromise beats fight, fight beats flight, and even fight is better than evading responsibility.** The point isn't to always reach consensus — it's to *choose* the response instead of letting your habit pick for you. Reaching consensus takes more time, but it's the only response that addresses the *underlying interests* of both parties rather than just splitting positions.

## Example — A workplace technical disagreement

You and a teammate disagree on which approach to use for a new feature: you want **library A**, they want **library B**. Each option plays out very differently:

- **🏃 Flight:** You say nothing, let them pick B, then quietly resent the decision and avoid the project. → Tech debt + interpersonal cost.
- **🥊 Fight:** You escalate to the team lead, push hard for A, "win" the call. → Library A is used, but your teammate disengages on future work.
- **🙇 Give up:** Mid-discussion you concede: *"Fine, do it your way."* → Library B is chosen, but you stop bringing strong technical opinions to future discussions.
- **🫥 Evade responsibility:** You punt it upward: *"Let's ask the team lead to decide."* The lead picks one without the full context. → Neither of you owns the outcome; trust erodes; if it fails, blame goes to the lead — not learning for either of you.
- **🤝 Compromise:** You use library A for one part and library B for another. → Works, but the codebase has two patterns and nobody's fully happy.
- **🎯 Consensus:** You stop arguing about *which library* and ask *what we actually need*: performance? team familiarity? long-term maintenance? Once interests are on the table, maybe library A wins clearly, or library B does, or a third option (an existing internal abstraction) emerges that neither of you considered. → Both feel heard, the decision is well-reasoned, and the team is stronger for next time.

**The trap most engineers fall into:** flipping between 🥊 Fight (in the meeting) and 🏃 Flight (afterwards, withdrawing). Naming this pattern is half the fix.

## Related Models

- [[feedback-model]] — Feedback Model handles *criticism received*; Conflict Resolution handles *open disagreement*. Both replace reaction with deliberate choice.
- [[rubber-band-model]] — Both surface what's really at stake (positions vs. underlying interests; fear-pulls vs. values-pulls).
- [[morphological-box-scamper]] — When stuck between two positions, SCAMPER can generate consensus options neither side started with.
- [[prisoners-dilemma]] — the payoff logic beneath the grid: consensus is cooperate/cooperate, fight is defect. PD warns *why* the elegant win/win is fragile — in a one-shot standoff it won't hold no matter how skilled you are; only a shared future makes cooperation rational → see [[choosing-cooperation]]
- [[role-playing-model]] — both break the autopilot: de Bono's hats add a de-escalation technique — get both parties wearing the *same* hat at once (all facts, then all caution, then all optimism) to route a stuck fight/flight standoff toward the shared-interest consensus this model prizes
