# The Double-Loop Learning Model

**Turn a mistake into a chance to question the *assumptions and goals* behind your action — not just to tweak the action itself.** *(Chris Argyris & Donald Schön.)*

## The Two Loops

```
   GOVERNING          ACTION /          RESULTS /
   ASSUMPTIONS  ───►   STRATEGY   ───►   CONSEQUENCES
        ▲                 ▲                   │
        │                 │                   │
        │                 └──── Loop 1 ───────┤   single: "am I doing things right?"
        │                    (fix the action) │      → adjust the action
        │                                     │
        └──────────────── Loop 2 ─────────────┘   double: "am I doing the RIGHT things?"
                   (revise the assumptions)          → adjust the assumptions
```

- **Single-loop** keeps your goals and assumptions fixed and only corrects the action — *"am I doing things right?"* It makes you more efficient at your current strategy.
- **Double-loop** steps back one level and interrogates the governing assumptions and goals that produced the action — *"am I doing the right things?"* It asks whether the strategy should exist at all.

## Second-Order Observation

Heinz von Foerster and Niklas Luhmann split **first-order observation** (watching the world) from **second-order observation** (watching the observer — including yourself). A first-order look at a mistake sees only what went wrong "out there." Second-order observation notices that *your own frame, categories and blind spots* helped produce the result: **the observer is part of the system being observed.** Double-loop learning is exactly this move applied to your own errors — you don't just watch the outcome, you watch the way of seeing that led you to act.

## How to Trigger the Second Loop

- When a result disappoints, ask: *"What did I have to assume was true for this action to seem right?"* — then challenge that assumption.
- Ask *"Am I doing the right things?"* before *"Am I doing things right?"*
- Ask why the goal itself exists — who set it, and would you choose it again today?
- Turn the observation on yourself: *"How did my own way of seeing help produce this result?"*

## When to Use It

- The same kind of mistake keeps recurring despite fixes — a sign you're only tightening the action, never the assumption
- A goal is being met perfectly but the results still feel wrong — question whether it's the right goal
- You catch yourself defending or explaining away an outcome rather than examining your own role in it
- High-stakes or novel situations where "do it better" isn't enough and the whole approach may be miscast

## Key Insight

Most mistakes get "solved" in the single loop: we work harder, tighten the process, fix the immediate action — and quietly protect the assumption that got us here. **The leverage is in the second loop.** The question that opens it: *what are the objectives and values behind your actions?* Once you're fully aware of those — not just the action, but the goals and beliefs driving it — you're able to change them. Because you are part of what produced the result, learning that lasts means turning the observation back on your own frame, not just on the world.

## Example

*Greta runs a weekly newsletter and open rates keep falling.*

- **Single-loop:** she assumes the goal (more opens) is right and just fixes the action — punchier subject lines, better send times, a fresh template. Rates blip up, then sag again.
- **Double-loop:** she steps back and questions the assumption itself — *"Why am I optimising opens at all? Do readers even want a weekly email?"* Watching her own frame, she sees she treated "newsletter" as the only option just because that's how she'd always done it. She kills the weekly send for a monthly deep-dive plus a chat community — and engagement, the thing she actually cared about, climbs.

- [[thinking-outside-the-box]] — an invented limit is a governing assumption; both fix the problem by questioning the frame, not retrying inside it
- [[swiss-cheese-model]] — after a failure, single-loop fixes the sharp-end slip; double-loop asks which governing assumptions left the holes aligned
- [[feedback-model]] — feedback is the trigger; single-loop just corrects the action, double-loop asks whether the goal behind it was wrong
- [[cognitive-dissonance-model]] — "change the attitude" is a single-loop dodge; double-loop resolves the tension by questioning the governing belief, not reframing it away
- [[appreciative-inquiry]] — flips the governing question from deficit ("what's broken?") to strengths ("what works?") — a double-loop reframe of problem-solving itself
- [[black-swan-model]] — the turkey that keeps refining its "humans feed me" model is stuck in the single loop; induction from past data can validate an assumption right up to catastrophe — the case for questioning the frame, not the estimate
- [[black-box-model]] — your governing assumptions are a black box you operate without inspecting: you see the inputs and outputs of your own behaviour but not the frame producing them — the second loop opens the lid
- [[role-playing-model]] — double-loop demands you question your own frame; switching hats or stepping into a role you'd avoid is the concrete lever that gets you *out* of it, not just noticing it
- [[changing-your-viewpoint]] — theme: questioning your governing assumptions is one move in the larger discipline of deliberately changing your own point of view
