# The Gap in the Market Model

**Plot everything that already exists on three axes, and the conspicuous empty region is where a potentially bankable idea sits — if it's empty for the right reason.**

Plot every rival as a point in a 3D cube, then look for the vacancy:

```
   x  cost-effectiveness   cheap/high-value ──► expensive/low-value
   y  prestige             low-status ──► premium
   z  awareness            obscure ──► famous

   ▪ ▪ ▪   existing offerings cluster here
      □     ← the conspicuous EMPTY region = the candidate gap

   Then ask: empty because nobody spotted it, or because nobody wants it?
```

## When to Use It
- Launching a new product, service, or brand into a crowded category and needing defensible positioning
- Hunting an underserved niche instead of fighting head-on in a packed segment
- Auditing competitors — a fast visual map of who occupies which space
- Sanity-checking an idea: is the space it targets genuinely empty, and *why*?

## Key Insight
The value isn't finding an empty spot — it's *interrogating* it. Mapping cost-effectiveness, prestige, and awareness shows you where competition is thin, but an empty space is only half the signal: **a gap is necessary but not sufficient.** Some gaps are empty because nobody has had the idea yet (real opportunity); many are empty because nobody *wants* what would go there, or it can't be delivered profitably. The one thing to remember: **a gap in the market is not the same as a market in the gap.** Always ask why the space is empty before you bet on it.

## Example
*Theo wants to launch a new cycling magazine. He lists every title on the shelf and plots each as a point in the cube. Two clusters appear: cheap mass-market weeklies — highly recognised but low-prestige — and expensive glossy quarterlies — high-prestige but barely known. The conspicuous vacancy is the mid-cost, high-prestige, high-awareness corner: an affordable magazine that still feels premium and could become widely recognised. That points to his idea — a mid-priced, beautifully produced monthly for everyday commuters who want the look of a premium title. But before committing, Theo applies the caution: is that corner empty because nobody spotted it, or because premium production at a mid-market price can't survive on the ad revenue a commuter title attracts? He runs a small subscription pre-sale to test whether the demand is actually there — rather than assuming the empty space guarantees buyers.*

- [[thinking-outside-the-box]] — both hunt value in unoccupied space (the nine-dot solution lives *around* the grid; a bankable idea in the empty cell); its real-vs-invented-limits distinction sharpens the core caution — take the gap held open by an invented limit, avoid the one held empty by real absence of demand
- [[swot-analysis]] — SWOT names an "Opportunity" quadrant but not how to find one; the Gap model is the concrete tool for locating that external opening — and before betting, SWOT your own strengths against it
- [[long-tail-model]] — explains *why* an empty region may or may not be a market: a gap can sit unfilled because serving that niche was too costly, not because demand is absent; when distribution cost falls toward zero, an unbankable gap can flip into a real one
- [[the-chasm-model]] — the downstream step: once the Gap model finds the opening and you build the offering, the Chasm tells you how to get it adopted — dominate one beachhead, then cross to the majority
