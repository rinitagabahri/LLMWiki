# The Hersey-Blanchard Model (Situational Leadership)

**There's no single best leadership style — match your style to the employee's competence and commitment on the task at hand, shifting instruct → coach → support → delegate as they grow.**

Two axes — how much you *direct* (task) and how much you *support* (relationship) — give four styles, each matched to the person's readiness (D1–D4):

```
              high  ┌─────────────────┬─────────────────┐
                    │ 3 SUPPORTING    │ 2 COACHING      │
   SUPPORTIVE       │ (participating) │ (selling)       │
  (relationship)    │ D3: skilled but │ D2: some skill, │
   behaviour        │ unsure          │ disillusioned   │
                    ├─────────────────┼─────────────────┤
                    │ 4 DELEGATING    │ 1 INSTRUCTING   │
                    │ D4: skilled &   │ (telling)       │
               low  │ self-reliant    │ D1: keen novice │
                    └─────────────────┴─────────────────┘
                      low          DIRECTIVE (task)  high
```

*As the person develops, the leader travels D1 → D2 → D3 → D4 (bottom-right → top-right → top-left → bottom-left) — pulling back direction, then support, as readiness rises.*

## When to Use It
- Onboarding a keen but green new hire — they need clear steps, not questions (**instruct**)
- A capable person who's hit reality and lost motivation or confidence (**coach**, then **support**)
- Someone competent and reliable who's ready for more autonomy — deciding how much to hand over (**delegate**)
- You're a one-size-fits-all manager, frustrated your usual approach lands with some people but not others

## Key Insight
Leadership is situational, not a fixed style. The commonest failure is applying one style — usually your favourite — to everyone: **delegating to a novice is abdication; instructing an expert is micromanagement.** And readiness is *task-specific*, not a personality label — the same person can be a self-reliant D4 on familiar work and a D1 the moment you hand them something new. Read the person *and* the task first, then choose.

## Example
*Leila manages Rahul, a new analyst. Week one he's eager but green, so she **instructs** — clear steps, tight check-ins (D1). A month in he's hit hard problems and looks deflated; she switches to **coaching** — still directive, but explaining the "why" and rebuilding his confidence (D2). By quarter's end he's technically solid but hesitant to make calls alone, so she **supports** — steps back on the how, asks his opinion, hands him ownership (D3). Within a year he runs his own projects, so she **delegates** — sets the goal and gets out of the way (D4). Yet on a brand-new tool he's never touched, she goes right back to **instructing** — proof that readiness is per-task, not a global label.*

## Related Models
- [[team-model]] — the Team Model's Roosevelt rule — pick good people, then "self-restraint enough to keep from meddling" — is exactly the **Delegating** style, but that's right only at the *ready* end; H-B generalises the meddle-vs-delegate call across every readiness level (a novice needs direction, not trust)
- [[personal-performance-model]] — its "Able to" and "Want to" are precisely the two readiness dimensions (competence + commitment) a leader reads to pick a style; H-B is the manager's-eye view of the same two axes
- [[john-whitmore-mode]] — GROW's answer is always *coach* (ask, don't tell); H-B says coaching is only right for the disillusioned learner — a clash that resolves on the person's readiness → see [[coach-or-adapt]]
- [[flow-model]] — a report's competence maps onto Flow's skill axis; a situational leader supplies from outside the "raise skill / adjust challenge" balance a person tunes for themselves
- [[feedback-model]] — the Coaching and Supporting styles are largely *delivered* through feedback; H-B tells a leader how directive to pitch it per readiness
- [[drexler-sibbet-team-performance]] — the group-vs-individual counterpart: Drexler/Sibbet builds the whole team through seven stages; H-B develops each person one at a time
- [[leading-a-team]] — theme: the individual counterpart to the leader's team-level jobs (like GROW, it develops one person rather than the group)
