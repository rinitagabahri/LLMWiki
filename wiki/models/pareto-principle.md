# The Pareto Principle

**Roughly 80% of your results come from 20% of your effort — inputs and outputs are lopsided, not linear, so a vital few actions carry most of the payoff.**

```
  INPUT                    OUTPUT
  ┌────┐                   ┌────────────────┐
  │20% │ ───the vital few──►│      80%       │
  └────┘                   └────────────────┘
  ┌────────────────┐       ┌────┐
  │      80%       │ ─trivial many─►│20% │
  └────────────────┘       └────┘

  Effort is NOT evenly productive: a small slice does most of the work.
```

*Vilfredo Pareto: 20% of Italy's population owned 80% of the land; 20% of his pea pods yielded 80% of the peas.*

## When to Use It

- Planning your time — find the 20% of tasks that produce most of the value, and do them first
- When everything feels equally urgent and demanding (it isn't — the load is lopsided)
- When "good enough" should beat "perfect" — knowing when to *stop* at ~80% instead of grinding the tail
- Diagnosing where results actually come from: which customers, features, clients, or habits are the vital few

## Key Insight

The counterintuitive part is that effort and reward aren't proportional. Your instinct assumes a fair, linear world — double the hours, double the result — but reality is skewed: a small share of inputs does most of the work, and the rest barely moves the needle. Two moves follow. **(1) Find the vital 20% and protect it** — do the high-leverage work first, before the trivial many crowd it out. **(2) Know when to stop** — on many tasks the first burst of effort captures ~80% of the value and the long tail is polish with diminishing returns. The numbers aren't literal (it can be 90/10 or 70/30); the point is the *asymmetry*, not the figure "80."

## Example

*Omar has a week and a long to-do list that all feels equally pressing. Instead of working top-to-bottom, he asks which items actually move his goals — and finds three of fifteen tasks (one key client proposal, one hiring decision, one product bug) will drive most of next quarter's outcome. He does those first, at full effort. The rest — inbox, formatting, a nice-to-have refactor — he deliberately does at "good enough," stopping at roughly 80% rather than polishing. He spends less total time and the quarter's results barely suffer, because the twelve small tasks were never where the value lived.*
