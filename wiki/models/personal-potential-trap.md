# The Personal Potential Trap

**The more potential you show, the faster expectations climb — until they outrun what you actually achieve, and the gap (not your real success) is what makes you feel you're never enough.**

## The Three Curves (over time)

```
 high ┤                        ⟋⟋  others' expectations of me
      │                     ⟋⟋⟋    my expectations of myself
      │                  ⟋⟋⟋
      │               ⟋⟋⟋      ↕  THE GAP
      │            ⟋⟋⟋   ______    ( = pressure, "never enough" )
      │         ⟋⟋__——‾‾  my actual achievements
      │     __——‾‾
 low  ┤——‾‾
      └──────────────────────────────→  time
```

| Curve | How it behaves |
|---|---|
| **My expectations of myself** | Climbs steeply — every success raises my own bar |
| **Other people's expectations of me** | Climbs even faster — praise and promise make others expect more |
| **My actual achievements** | Rises too, but more slowly, and eventually flattens |

The two expectation curves pull ahead of the achievement curve, and the **gap between them widens over time**.

## When to Use It

- When you're objectively succeeding but still feel like you're falling short
- When early praise ("so much potential") set a bar you've been chasing ever since
- When others' expectations (a parent, a boss, a mentor) feel crushing rather than motivating
- To diagnose burnout or impostor feelings that don't match your real track record

## Key Insight

Potential is a *trap* because expectations rise faster than achievement ever can — and **what determines whether you feel successful is the gap, not your actual performance.** You can't close it by achieving more: each new achievement just resets expectations higher. The only real exit is downward on the *expectation* curve, not upward on the achievement one — so **promise 80, deliver 120**: deliberately set expectations below what you can do, flipping the gap in your favour instead of forever chasing it.

## Example

*Johan was the "high-potential" hire — flagged as a future lead in his first month. Each win raised the bar: ship a feature, and now he's expected to ship two; lead a project, and now he's expected to lead the team. His achievements are genuinely climbing — promotions, real impact — yet he feels like a failure, because his own and his manager's expectations always run a step ahead. The trap: he keeps trying to close the gap by achieving more, which only lifts expectations again. The model shows him the gap is structural, not a personal shortfall. The fix isn't a bigger achievement — it's an honest conversation to recalibrate expectations (his and his manager's) to what's actually sustainable.*

## Related Models

- [[cognitive-dissonance-model]] — the gap between expectation and reality is a dissonance you resolve by lowering expectations or reframing, not just "trying harder"
- [[flow-model]] — when expectation (challenge) runs far ahead of achievement (skill), you're pushed into the anxiety zone
- [[feedback-model]] — others' expectations are feedback you can accept-and-change, accept-and-keep, or *reject* — you don't have to internalise every one
- [[uffe-elbaek-model]] — the split between how you see yourself and how others see you is exactly the two expectation curves
- [[personal-performance-model]] — a flat "want to" under high "have to" is often this trap showing up day to day
- [[maslow-pyramid]] — chasing others' esteem is a deficiency need achievement can't fill; Maslow's exit is self-actualisation, not more approval
