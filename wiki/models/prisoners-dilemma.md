# The Prisoner's Dilemma

**Two people each acting in their own rational self-interest can end up worse off than if they'd simply trusted each other — which is exactly why, in any relationship that repeats, trust is the smarter move.**

Two suspects are held separately. Each can stay silent (**cooperate**) or betray the other (**defect**). Sentences, lower = better:

```
                    They COOPERATE        They DEFECT
  You COOPERATE     both: 1 yr            you: 3 / them: 0
  You DEFECT        you: 0 / them: 3      both: 2 yrs
```

- Both cooperate → 1 yr each (good for both)
- Both defect → 2 yrs each (bad for both)
- Lone defector goes free (0); the lone cooperator takes the hit (3) — the "sucker"

Whatever the other does, defecting looks better *for you* — so both defect, and both land on 2 years, worse than the 1 they'd have shared by trusting. **Individually smart, collectively stupid.**

## When to Use It
- Both sides could gain by cooperating, but each is tempted to grab a short-term edge (price wars, negotiations, splitting credit or cost)
- Deciding how far to trust someone — first ask: is this a *one-shot* or a *repeated* relationship?
- Designing incentives so cooperation is the smart individual choice (contracts, team norms, partnerships)
- You've been burned and need a response rule instead of a feud

## Key Insight
Trust becomes rational the moment the game **repeats**. With a stranger you'll never meet again, betrayal pays. But most real relationships are iterated — same colleague, supplier, neighbour, again and again — and this "shadow of the future" means today's betrayal invites tomorrow's revenge. Axelrod's tournaments found the winning strategy was the simplest: **tit-for-tat** — cooperate first, then copy the other's last move. It works because it's **nice** (never defects first), **retaliatory** (punishes betrayal at once, so it's not a pushover), **forgiving** (returns to cooperation the moment the other does), and **clear** (predictable, not out to "win"). So the answer to *why trust someone?* isn't optimism — it's that in repeated dealings, being trustworthy reliably beats grabbing the short-term prize.

## Example
*Dara and Bianca own competing cafés on the same block. Each can hold prices (cooperate) or slash them to poach the other's regulars (defect). Both hold → both keep healthy margins. Both discount → they gut each other's profit, the worst outcome. For a single busy weekend, undercutting tempts. But they're neighbours indefinitely — an iterated game. The first time Bianca cuts prices, Dara matches within a day; the war hurts them both. Over months they settle into an unspoken tit-for-tat: hold, match any cut, forgive once the other pulls back. Both stay profitable. Trusting the rival to hold isn't sentimental — it's the rational equilibrium of a game that repeats.*

## Related Models
- [[conflict-resolution-model]] — its six responses are the moves inside a two-party standoff (consensus = cooperate/cooperate, fight = defect); PD explains *why* the win/win is fragile and when it becomes rational — only a shared future makes it hold → see [[choosing-cooperation]]
- [[esquire-gift-model]] — generous giving is an opening cooperative move; over repeated rounds it builds the reciprocity and reputation PD says make trust pay — "err generous" is a tit-for-tat-friendly rule, not just etiquette
- [[black-box-model]] — both turn a decision into a trust question: the Black Box asks whether to trust an opaque system's *competence*, PD asks when to trust a self-interested party's *intentions* — and answers: when the game repeats
- [[consequences-model]] — the one-shot dilemma is a choice made without seeing the other's move; decide anyway, but know that repetition (not more information) is what makes trust safe
- [[network-target-model]] — the shadow of the future lives in your inner rings: the daily/weekly people are repeated-game partners where cooperation pays, while rim acquaintances behave more like one-shot games
- [[drexler-sibbet-team-performance]] — a team is the ultimate *repeated* game, so the leader daring the first cooperative move (vulnerability, not just calculation) is what actually gets trust started
