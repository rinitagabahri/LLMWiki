# Theme: Deciding Under Uncertainty

Several models share one uncomfortable truth: **the future can't be computed exactly — only approximated.** Each teaches the same discipline from a different angle — reason in *probabilities and ranges*, separate the quality of a decision from the luck of its outcome, and act despite incomplete information rather than chasing a certainty that doesn't exist.

## Models in This Theme

| Model | What it says about uncertainty |
|---|---|
| [[monte-carlo-simulation]] | Makes it explicit — sample the unknowns thousands of times and read the *distribution*; the answer is a range with odds, not a point |
| [[consequences-model]] | Outcomes are unforecastable, so decide on minimal information and course-correct — waiting for certainty forfeits the option |
| [[hype-cycle]] | The curve can't tell you *which* technologies reach the plateau; don't mistake peak-noise or trough-despair for the verdict |
| [[swiss-cheese-model]] | Failure is the improbable *alignment* of independent holes — think in compound probabilities, blame the system not the last person |
| [[black-swan-model]] | The events that matter most are unpredictable outliers — induction from the past can't foresee them; plan for fragility, not forecasts |

## Synthesis

Put together, they move from *stance* → *method*:

- **Consequences** sets the stance: stop waiting for a certainty that never arrives; a good decision is judged by its reasoning, not its (partly random) result.
- **Monte Carlo** gives the method: when you can't compute the answer, simulate it — turn "I don't know" into "here's the range and the odds."
- **Hype Cycle** applies it to *time*: the same technology looks like a triumph at the peak and a flop in the trough; only the spread of futures is real.
- **Swiss Cheese** applies it to *failure*: rare events happen when independent low-probability holes line up, so manage the system's odds, not the last mistake.
- **Black Swan** marks the theme's outer limit: the first four handle *known unknowns* (quantifiable variance inside a model you trust), but the Black Swan is the *unknown unknown* — the reminder that the model's structure itself can be wrong, so even an honest distribution can miss the one event that dominates everything.

The common move is to **replace a fake single number with an honest distribution** — and then act anyway. The opposite failure mode is *false precision*: a confident point-estimate ("it'll cost £63,700," "this will take 15 days") that hides how little you actually know. And the sharpest edge of that trap — trusting a distribution *as if* it bounded reality — is exactly where [[black-swan-vs-monte-carlo]] picks up.
